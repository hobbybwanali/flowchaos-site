User guide

Design tools

Size pipes automatically, check fire flow, draw long sections and set design levels.

Automatic pipe sizing

Analysis > Automatic Pipe Sizing finds the cheapest sizes of a pipe series that keep the network within your limits. It works in stages, each with the advanced solver:

  1. Feasibility: with every pipe at its largest size, junctions still short of pressure are limited by the source level. They are reported (a pump or a higher tank is needed) and not chased.
  2. Sizes from flows: the flows at peak demand give each pipe the smallest size that carries its flow below the velocity limit.
  3. Repair: pipes that are too fast or lose too much head go up a size; where junctions are short of pressure, the pipes on the paths that supply them go up where a size gains the most head for its extra cost.
  4. Cost reduction: each pipe is tried one size smaller, largest savings first, and kept smaller only where every limit still holds.

Pipes with the field size_locked = yes keep their size. A main that fills a tank is sized for the demand the tank supplies (in a steady run a tank fills as fast as its inlet allows, which is not a design flow).

Results

The table shows each pipe's current and new size, nominal size, flow, velocity, head loss and a note. The summary gives the cost of the design (from the series' prices, or a relative cost), lengths by size, the lowest pressure, and the pipes and junctions that need attention. Apply Sizes writes diameter, roughness, material, pressure class and nominal size in one step that can be undone.

Add prices to a series to see real costs: see reference data.

Fire flow analysis

Analysis > Fire Flow Analysis gives, for every hydrant, the largest flow it can deliver while its residual pressure and the pressure at every other junction stay above the minimums. Test every junction, the selected ones, or those whose hydrant field is yes. The table shows the static pressure, available flow, residual pressure and the node that limits each hydrant; Select Failing Hydrants shows on the map where the network needs strengthening.

How it works: for each hydrant the fire flow is added as an extra withdrawal and the network is solved again, raising the flow until a pressure limit is reached (a search that needs only a few runs). The model stays open between runs, so even large networks finish quickly, and Stop ends the analysis at once, keeping the hydrants already tested.

Criticality analysis

Analysis > Criticality Analysis asks, for each pipe, pump or valve: what if it were out, from a burst or a repair? Each tested link is closed in turn.

  1. Junctions that lose every path to a reservoir or tank are cut off: all their withdrawal is lost.
  2. The rest of the network is solved with pressure-dependent withdrawal: a junction below the service pressure delivers only part of its withdrawal, and nothing at or below the pressure where delivery stops. What it misses beyond its normal shortfall counts as lost too.
Setting Meaning
Test every pipe, the selected links, or every pipe, pump and valve
Smallest pipe tested skip service pipes below this diameter on large networks
Service pressure full withdrawal at or above this pressure
Delivery stops at no withdrawal at or below this pressure
At design withdrawal demand patterns held at 1, times the peak factor; off, the patterns at the start time

Each link is rated by the share of the total withdrawal not delivered while it is out: High from 20 %, Medium from 5 %, Low from 0.5 %, otherwise None. The table also shows the junctions cut off, the shortfall and the lowest pressure that remains. Double-click a row to find the link; Select High and Medium selects them all; Show on Map colours the links by criticality (the Criticality link view, kept with the project); Export CSV lists the affected junctions too.

Reservoirs and tanks keep supplying for the moment analysed, so a pump that fills a tank may show no effect: the tank covers the outage until it runs down. Use an extended period run to see how long.

Long sections

Select pipes and choose L-Profile (Ctrl+Shift+P). The profile shows ground, pipe invert, minimum cover and the hydraulic grade line, with tables of levels, depth, cover and chainage and the excavation quantities. It follows the selection on the map.

The sheet is drawn at the Horizontal scale on screen chosen above it (1:2,000 by default; 1:500 to 1:50,000, or Fit to window) and is as long as the pipeline at that scale: it scrolls sideways, and Ctrl with the mouse wheel zooms along it. The settings are on the left; Build Profile, Export (PDF, PNG or the chainage table as CSV) and Close stay at the foot of the window.

The values never overlap: where chainages crowd together they are spread apart with a leader line back to their true place, as in the planning network diagram, and node names do the same. If even that leaves no room the sheet says how many node values were left out (the CSV table has them all).

  • Vertical exaggeration: fit the sheet, or fix it (for example 10:1) when the sheet has room.
  • Chainage as km+m: 1+234.5 instead of 1234.5.
  • High and low points: peaks of the pipe (for air valves) and dips (for washouts) are marked; fittings already on the map are filled, points found from the levels are open.
  • Quantities box: in the header, in a corner of the chart, or dragged to any place on the sheet.

The trench is the pipe's outside diameter plus the trench allowance wide, and reaches the bedding depth below the invert; excavation is summed along the profile from these. The grade line is drawn between every node that has a result, so a node left out of the analysis does not remove it.

Design levels

Design Levels sets invert levels from the ground and a cover depth, or tank stand heights, for the selected nodes.

Area Demand Allocator

Design > Area Demand Allocator (also in the Irrigation menu and the Processing Toolbox) sets the design withdrawals of junctions from the polygons they supply: the blocks of an irrigation scheme, the zones of a town. The settings are on the left and the matches on the right; Match, Apply and Close stay at the foot of the window.

The areas are measured from the polygons themselves (holes left out, on the ellipsoid like the areas of a GIS), in hectares, square metres, acres or square kilometres, so the layer needs no area field. The demand of each area comes from one of two bases:

  • A rate for each unit of area (unit area loading): its area times the rate, for example the peak irrigation requirement in l/s per hectare, and a factor.
  • A total flow shared by area: the scheme's design flow shared among the areas in proportion to their areas, times the factor.

Tick Share by a number stored for each area to use a field instead of the measured area, for example a population or a number of plots (the attribute table's field calculator can work one out first). The withdrawals are rounded to the Decimal places chosen (2 by default), in the table and when set.

The demand of each area goes to its junctions by one of the methods of the design literature:

  • The junction in each area (nearest node): the junction inside it nearest its centre; with none inside, the junction nearest the area within the search distance.
  • Shared equally among the junctions in each area (equal distribution).
  • Each junction takes the area it lies in.
  • Thiessen service areas: each junction supplies the part of the areas nearer to it than to any other junction (Voronoi polygons), so an area's demand is split by the share of it each junction serves.
  • Shared by half the length of the pipes at each junction (the unit length method): a junction with more pipe around it takes a larger share of the area's demand.

Match lists every area (numbered as in its layer) with its area, its withdrawal, the junction's current withdrawal, its junction (a shared area shows each junction's share) and how it was found, and draws an arrow on the map from each area to its junction, coloured by how it was found; the colours of inside, nearest outside, set by hand and no junction can be chosen. Change a match in the table, select a junction on the map and Use Selected Junction, or drag on the map: an arrow's head dropped on another junction gives the area to that junction, and an end dropped inside another area has that junction supply that area instead; the table follows. Clear the Map takes the arrows and highlights off the map. The summary counts what was found and names junctions that supply more than one area, to check.

Apply replaces the design withdrawals (or adds to them), and can set the junctions that supply no area to zero; it is one step of Undo. A banner above the table says how it went: green when every junction has its new withdrawal (shown in green under Current), amber when some areas had no junction, red with the junctions that could not be set.

Irrigation

The Irrigation menu holds sprinkler, drip and centre pivot design tools that start from crop water needs and soil and draw the layout on the map.

Each design tool has a Concept drawing page beside its report: the field to scale with the mainline or manifold, the laterals, the sprinklers and their wetted circles or the drip rows, the blocks watered one after another, the source and pump, dimensions, a legend, a north arrow, a scale bar and a title block. Choose the land shape (rectangle, L-shaped, tapering, triangle, irregular; for a pivot a circle in a square field, a full or a half circle): every shape keeps the area of the designed field. Export it as PNG or PDF (A3); a saved report includes it.

Quick Design Estimates gives a first look from a few inputs: for drip the daily need reduced for ground cover, the emitters, the application rate, the watering time and the blocks that fit the hours available; for sprinklers the depth the soil and roots hold, the interval, the precipitation rate (checked against the soil's intake), the set time, the sprinklers running together and the pump flow. Irrigation > Climate Data reads monthly climate records (.clim) for crop water needs, and Apply Climate to Withdrawals turns them into the withdrawals of the irrigated junctions.

The climate window is a sheet of the twelve months: each month's reference evapotranspiration and rainfall, with the effective rainfall and the net need worked out, and the peak month marked. Type the values, or paste them from a spreadsheet or a text (tab, comma or semicolon separated, with or without a month column); New Climate Sheet starts a blank one, Save Climate File keeps it as a .clim file to use again, and Sample Data loads the Lilongwe means after asking before it replaces entries. Choose a month and FlowCHAOS scales the outlets for it; the design flow is kept with the project. Sample Projects has a sprinkler scheme in July with the Lilongwe (Chitedze) climate to try it with.
